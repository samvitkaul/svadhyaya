# From irrational square roots to norms and geometries

## The path of the question

A geometric argument for the irrationality of $\sqrt2$ led to: can we prove the irrationality of $\sqrt p$ for each prime $p$ in a reusable way? What does Pell's equation mean beyond recreational number theory? Then a further question: if complex numbers $a+bi$ have a norm-one circle, what curve arises from $a+b\sqrt2$?

## A shared algebraic construction

For a quadratic extension $K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt D)$ with squarefree $D$, conjugation sends $a+b\sqrt D$ to $a-b\sqrt D$, giving the field norm

$$N(a+b\sqrt D)=a^2-Db^2.$$

Thus the norm-one locus $a^2-Db^2=1$ is a hyperbola for $D>0$. In $\mathbb Q(i)$, the norm $a^2+b^2=1$ gives a circle. When $a,b$ are integers and $D>0$ is a nonsquare integer, $a^2-Db^2=1$ becomes **Pell's equation**.

## The subtle correction

The norm-one hyperbola does **not** by itself endow the plane with hyperbolic geometry. It is naturally related to the split orthogonal group $O(1,1)$, hyperbolic rotations and Lorentzian quadratic forms. Likewise, the circle is a level set of a positive-definite form, rather than a construction of all Euclidean geometry from the symbol $i$.

The signature of the quadratic form is the pivotal geometric feature. More general field extensions produce higher-degree norm forms, but not automatically a catalogue of constant-curvature geometries.
